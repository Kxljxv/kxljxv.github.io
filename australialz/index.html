The Australian System of Government

Discover how Australia is governed: explore the unique blend of constitutional monarchy, federalism, and parliamentary democracy that shapes the nation.

Australia at a Glance

Australia's system of government is founded on liberal democratic principles, such as religious freedom, freedom of speech, and the rule of law. It draws from British and North American influences, but has evolved its own distinctive practices and institutions.

The Commonwealth of Australia was established in 1901, uniting six former British colonies into a federal nation. Today, Australia is made up of six states and two self-governing territories. Each state and territory has its own constitution, parliament, government, and laws, but all are united under the Australian Constitution.

  • Federation: Australia is a federation, meaning power is shared between a national government and state/territory governments. Other federations include Canada, India, and Germany.
  • Parliamentary Government: The Executive Government—ministers and Prime Minister—must be drawn from the Parliament and is responsible to it.
  • Constitutional Monarchy: The King is the formal head of state, represented in Australia by the Governor-General, but holds mainly ceremonial powers.

The Australian Constitution (effective 1901) is the supreme law that sets out the structure of government, powers of each branch, and how they interact. Some important features of government are based on tradition and political practice, not just the written Constitution.

The system is designed with checks and balances to prevent any one part of government from holding too much power, and to ensure accountability to the people.

Australia's States & Territories

Australia consists of six states and two self-governing territories, each with its own parliament, government, and unique responsibilities. The federal capital is Canberra, in the Australian Capital Territory. Each state and territory is responsible for a range of services, such as health, education, police, and local government regulation.

Each state and territory maintains its own legal system, and differences in their responsibilities reflect their unique histories and needs.

The Three Layers of Government in Australia

Australia’s system of government is organised into three interdependent layers: the federal (national) government, state/territory governments, and local governments. Each layer has its own powers, responsibilities, and institutions, and each plays a crucial role in governing the country.

Federal (Commonwealth) Government

The federal government is responsible for matters that affect the entire nation. Its powers are set out in the Constitution and include:

  • Defence and military
  • Foreign affairs and trade
  • Immigration and citizenship
  • Currency and banking
  • Social security and national welfare
  • National taxation and customs
The seat of the federal government is in Canberra, ACT.

State and Territory Governments

State and territory governments are responsible for areas not managed by the federal government, including:

  • Public hospitals and health services
  • Public education (schools and TAFEs)
  • Police, fire, ambulance, and emergency services
  • Roads, public transport, and land use
  • Regulation and funding of local government
Each state and territory has its own constitution, parliament, and courts. States have more autonomy; territories have powers delegated by the federal parliament.

Local Governments

Local governments (councils or shires) are created by state governments and are responsible for:

  • Town planning and zoning
  • Building codes and permits
  • Local roads, parks, and community facilities
  • Waste collection and recycling
  • Water, sewerage, and drainage (in some areas)
  • Local events and recreational services
There are about 900 local governments in Australia. Their powers differ by state, and they are funded by local rates as well as state and federal grants.

Why Three Layers?

Australia’s three-layer system allows for both national consistency (through the federal government) and local flexibility (through states, territories, and councils). States reflect Australia’s colonial history and regional diversity, while local governments provide services tailored to the needs of specific communities.

Separation of Powers

The Australian Constitution establishes a system of government based on the separation of powers. This means that the responsibilities of governing are divided among three branches, each with its own area of authority:

  • Legislative: The Parliament makes and changes the laws. This includes the House of Representatives and the Senate at the federal level, and similar bodies in each state and territory.
  • Executive: The Prime Minister, Cabinet, and government departments put the laws into action. The Governor-General (representing the King) formally approves laws and appoints ministers, but acts on the advice of the elected government.
  • Judiciary: The High Court and other federal courts interpret the laws and ensure they are applied fairly. Courts have the power to rule on the validity of laws and to settle disputes.

Why is this important? The separation of powers is designed to prevent any one branch from becoming too powerful and to provide checks and balances. For example, the courts can rule a law invalid if it goes against the Constitution, and Parliament can scrutinise the actions of the Executive.

In practice, there is some overlap, especially between the Parliament and the Executive. Ministers must be members of Parliament, meaning the legislative and executive roles are not entirely separate. However, Australia’s system still maintains vital checks and balances—especially through the independence of the judiciary and the existence of an official opposition in Parliament.

How Elections Work in Australia

Elections are a cornerstone of Australian democracy, ensuring that government at every level is accountable to the people. Australians regularly vote to choose representatives at the federal, state/territory, and local levels. Each level of government has its own electoral system and cycle.

Federal Elections

Federal Parliament consists of two houses: the House of Representatives (lower house) and the Senate (upper house). Federal elections are held at least every three years.

  • House of Representatives: Each of the 151 electorates elects one member, using a system called preferential voting. The party (or coalition) with a majority of members forms the government, and its leader becomes Prime Minister.
  • Senate: Each state elects 12 senators, and each territory elects two, using proportional representation. This system gives all states equal representation and often allows minor parties to win seats.
  • All Australian citizens aged 18 and over are required to vote. Voting is by secret ballot.

State and Territory Elections

Each state and territory has its own parliament and electoral system. Most states have two houses (lower and upper), but Queensland, the Northern Territory, and the ACT have only one. Voting is compulsory in all states and territories except Western Australia local councils.

  • Lower houses: Elect government, usually using preferential voting.
  • Upper houses: Where they exist, use proportional or preferential systems.
  • State and territory elections are usually held every four years.

Local Government Elections

Local government (council) elections are run by state electoral commissions. Councillors and, in some places, mayors are elected to represent their local communities. Voting rules vary between states—compulsory in some (e.g., Victoria, NSW), voluntary in others (e.g., WA).

  • Local councils decide on community issues like planning, local roads, parks, and waste.
  • Voting systems vary: often preferential or proportional voting.

Referendums

The Australian Constitution can only be changed by referendum. This means a proposed change must be approved by a majority of voters nationally and a majority in at least four of the six states. Referendums are rare and require extensive public debate.

Why Are Elections Important?

Elections ensure that government is responsible to the people and reflect the will of the community. Compulsory voting helps achieve high turnouts and broad representation. Australia's robust electoral system is designed to maintain fairness, transparency, and public confidence at every level of government.
