Australia at a Glance

  • System: A federal parliamentary constitutional monarchy based on the liberal democratic tradition, with a written Constitution since 1901.
  • Levels: Three main levels – Federal (Commonwealth), State/Territory, and Local governments.
  • Parliamentary government: The Executive comes from, and is responsible to, the Parliament.
  • Constitutional Monarchy: The King is Head of State (represented by the Governor-General), but real power is exercised by elected officials.

Layers of Government: Federal, State/Territory, Local

  • Federal (Country): Makes laws for the whole nation (defence, immigration, foreign policy, trade, currency, etc.).
  • State/Territory: Each state/territory has its own parliament and constitution. Responsible for health, education, roads, police, emergency services, and local government regulation.
  • Local: Councils or shires. Control town planning, local roads, waste, water, community facilities, and building codes. Funded by rates and state/federal grants.

Why have all three layers?
States/Territories reflect historical colonies and help tailor laws to local needs (e.g. education, hospitals). Local government delivers close-to-home services and community priorities. The three levels work together, but each has distinct powers defined by the Constitution and state laws.

Separation of Powers

  • Legislative: Makes laws (Parliament: House of Representatives & Senate)
  • Executive: Puts laws into action (Prime Minister, Cabinet, Departments, Governor-General)
  • Judiciary: Interprets and applies the law (High Court & Federal Courts)

Note: In Australia, executive ministers must also be members of Parliament, so there is overlap. Checks still exist via the bicameral Parliament and an independent judiciary.

Parliament, Cabinet, and the Executive

  • Parliament: House of Representatives (forms government), Senate (reviews/amends laws). Both must pass laws.
  • Executive: Cabinet (Prime Minister + Ministers), implements laws and manages government.
  • Governor-General: Represents the King, formally signs laws and appoints Ministers, acts on advice of Cabinet.

After elections, the party (or coalition) with the most seats in the House of Representatives forms government. The Cabinet is the main policy-making body, while the Governor-General’s role is mostly ceremonial but carries constitutional significance.
